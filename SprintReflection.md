# Sprint 12 Reflection — Earnings Intelligence Engine

## What was built

A deterministic Earnings Intelligence domain that answers "how did the company perform in its latest reported period, and what changed?" — the question none of Sprints 1-11 answered directly (raw statements have no comparison, 5-year Business Analysis is CAGR-oriented, Alerts only fire on threshold breaches). `backend/earnings/` (periods, calculator, signals, market reaction, validator, formatter, service, controller, routes — 9 files plus `__tests__/`), one API endpoint (`GET /api/earnings/:ticker`) returning growth/profitability/cash-flow/balance-sheet/per-share comparisons, three rule-based earnings-quality observations, FCF Conversion, deterministic Improving/Stable/Deteriorating signals, market reaction (with an explicitly disclosed methodology — see below), and related Earnings-category news. A frontend Earnings tab (`frontend/src/components/Earnings.jsx` + `frontend/src/components/earnings/`) added to the Company Intelligence nav between Valuation and News. 82 new backend tests and 8 new frontend tests (zero regressions across 726 backend / 106 frontend tests total), and 13 documentation files across `research/finance/`, `research/engineering/`, and `research/product/`. The optional AI narration endpoint (`POST /api/earnings/:ticker/explain`) was scoped in detail but deliberately not built this sprint — see "What was intentionally deferred."

## Finance concepts learned

The most consequential finance lesson was that "earnings" and "net income" are not synonyms, and building the calculator forced that distinction to be concrete rather than aspirational — FCF Conversion, the Net-Income-vs-FCF quality observation, and the Debt-vs-Cash-Flow observation exist specifically because a company can show growing net income while its cash generation tells a different story, and only looking across statements (Income Statement, Balance Sheet, Cash Flow Statement together) surfaces that. The second lesson was the percentage-point vs. relative-percent distinction (`MarginAnalysis.md`) — it's easy to state as a rule but easy to violate by accident in code, which is why `marginPointChange` is a structurally separate function from `percentChange` rather than the same function with a flag, and why the frontend formatter renders them with visibly different suffixes (`"pp"` vs `"%"`).

## Engineering concepts learned

The clearest engineering decision was reuse discipline over convenience: every margin/ROE/ROA/FCF formula in `earnings.calculator.js` calls `ratio.formulas.js` rather than reimplementing it, which meant inheriting a real, already-fixed sign-handling bug's regression coverage for free rather than risking reintroducing it. The signal thresholds followed the same discipline — `earnings.signals.js` imports `THRESHOLDS` from `alert.rules.js` directly for every metric where an existing number already defined "meaningful" (margin points, FCF decline, debt increase), rather than inventing parallel numbers that could quietly drift out of sync with what the Alert Engine considers significant for the same data. The one place a genuinely new threshold was needed (raw growth-rate magnitude, which no existing rule measured) is documented inline with why no reuse was possible.

## Product decisions

Zero charts shipped, per the brief's explicit "avoid information overload" instruction — a single grouped comparison table, a short observations list, and two stat tiles for market reaction cover every required metric. Balance-sheet items (debt, cash, net debt) deliberately never get an Improving/Deteriorating label, only a neutral ↑/↓/→ direction, because the brief is explicit that debt in isolation isn't inherently good or bad. The optional AI narration layer was scoped but not built, so the deterministic feature could ship complete and trustworthy on its own rather than half-gated behind an unfinished AI layer.

## Why earnings analysis is different from financial statements

A financial statement is a table of facts with no reference point; earnings analysis is the same facts *plus* a comparison, a margin read, a quality observation, and a signal — the interpretive layer that turns "what happened" into "what changed and does it matter." Sprint 1's Financial Statements page and Sprint 12's Earnings page can show the exact same underlying numbers and still answer completely different questions.

## Period comparison decisions

Investigation confirmed Athena's financial data is annual-only — the Yahoo provider (`yahooFinance.provider.js`) requests only `annual*` timeseries fields, Twelve Data's provider requests no `period=quarterly` parameter, and `FinancialStatement` has no quarter field at all. Rather than fabricate a quarterly comparison or silently pretend the limitation doesn't exist, `earnings.periods.js` threads an explicit `periodType` (`"ANNUAL"` today; `"QUARTERLY"`/`"TTM"` reserved in the enum) and `comparisonType` (`"YoY"`) through the entire response, so the architecture is quarterly-ready without any component needing to change when that data eventually exists.

## Earnings quality decisions

Three specific relationships (Net Income vs. FCF, Revenue growth vs. profit growth, Debt vs. cash flow) rather than a single composite score — the brief is explicit that an arbitrary blended score would hide which underlying relationship actually matters, and there's no defensible weighting scheme for combining them into one number. Every observation is generated from a fixed template driven by the sign/magnitude of an already-computed value; no LLM is involved in this section, and no observation is ever phrased as an accusation ("moved in different directions," never "signals manipulation").

## Market reaction decisions

The hardest genuinely underspecified design call this sprint: Athena has no earnings-release date anywhere in its data. Approximating with fiscal-year-end was rejected as actively misleading (10-Ks file 60-90 days after FYE, so that window would measure the wrong thing entirely). The solution anchors on the most recent stored Earnings-category news article instead — reusing Sprint 10's News domain rather than adding a second date source — and labels the result explicitly (`"basis": "most recent Earnings-category news article"`), never as "the earnings release date." Verified against real cached `MarketHistory` data before considering it done, not just against mocks.

## AI architecture

Fully designed, not implemented (see `research/engineering/GroundedEarningsExplanation.md`): a narrow `{narrative, evidenceUsed}` JSON schema, a closed evidence allow-list built from the exact deterministic payload `GET /api/earnings/:ticker` already returns, and the same retry-once-on-validation-failure pattern Sprint 8's `ai.service.js` already proved out. The deferral was a deliberate scope decision, confirmed explicitly mid-sprint rather than assumed — the deterministic API is complete and independently valuable without it.

## What was intentionally deferred

The optional AI narration endpoint (`POST /api/earnings/:ticker/explain` — designed, not built, per an explicit checkpoint during the sprint); quarterly and TTM earnings support (blocked by a genuine data-source gap, not effort — see Period comparison decisions); non-adjacent-year comparison labeling (`resolvePeriods` pairs the two most recent statements by year and labels the result "YoY" even across a data gap — a known, documented limitation, not a silent one); and any product-analytics instrumentation (page views, comparison-table interaction, alert-to-earnings conversion — described as worth measuring in `EarningsIntelligenceProductDesign.md` §10, not implemented without real usage to justify it).

## Challenges

Deciding the market reaction anchor date methodology was the sprint's central design challenge — there was no existing Athena precedent to reuse directly (unlike almost everything else in this sprint), so it required weighing a materially-wrong-but-simple option (fiscal year-end) against a proxy-but-honest option (most recent Earnings news) and choosing to disclose the proxy explicitly rather than presenting either as ground truth. A smaller challenge was keeping `earnings.formatter.js`'s response shape stable across phases — designing `marketReaction`'s eventual shape (`available/reason/basis/anchorDate/oneDayReturnPercent/fiveDayReturnPercent`) during Phase 4, before Phase 7 actually computed it, meant the formatter and frontend needed no changes at all once the real market reaction module landed.

## Future improvements

Quarterly earnings support once Athena's financial data providers are extended to fetch `quarterly*` fields (the `periodType` enum and `resolvePeriods` architecture are already built for this — see Period comparison decisions). A confidence indicator on market reaction proportional to how many days elapsed between the anchor news article and the actual (unknown) report date, if that gap ever becomes measurable. The AI narration endpoint, using the grounding design already written up in `GroundedEarningsExplanation.md`. Detecting and explicitly flagging non-adjacent-year comparisons in `earnings.periods.js` rather than silently labeling any two-statement pairing "YoY."

## Finance interview questions

1. **"Why doesn't Athena treat 'earnings' as synonymous with net income?"** — Because net income alone can hide a materially important story — a company can grow net income while free cash flow deteriorates, or grow revenue while margin quietly contracts. Athena's Earnings Intelligence deliberately covers growth, profitability, margins, cash flow, balance sheet, and per-share metrics together, because each answers a question the others can't.
2. **"Walk through how Athena computes FCF Conversion when net income is negative."** — `fcfConversion(freeCashFlow, netIncome)` still computes `freeCashFlow / netIncome` (a real, valid division) and attaches a static caveat explaining that the ratio's usual "cents of cash per dollar of profit" reading doesn't apply with a negative denominator — it is never hidden and never labeled fraudulent or problematic, per the brief's explicit instruction.
3. **"Why does a margin change get reported in percentage points instead of a relative percent?"** — Because the two describe genuinely different things and conflating them misleads: 30.1%→28.4% is "-1.7 percentage points," but computed as a relative percent it would read as roughly "-5.6%," a number that sounds like a much larger move than what actually happened.
4. **"Athena only has annual financial data — why not just show a fake quarterly comparison using the annual number divided by 4?"** — That would be fabricating data Athena doesn't have and presenting it as if it were real reported figures — the brief is explicit: "do NOT fabricate quarterly." The architecture (periodType enum) is built so real quarterly data can be added later; a synthetic approximation would actively mislead in the meantime.
5. **"How does Athena avoid claiming earnings caused a stock's price move?"** — The Market Reaction module and its UI copy are written entirely in observational language ("shares moved X% following [date]"), never causal language ("earnings caused shares to move"). The anchor date itself is explicitly labeled as a proxy (the most recent Earnings-category news article date), not presented as verified ground truth.
6. **"What's the difference between Athena's Earnings Intelligence and its 5-year Business Analysis (Sprint 3)?"** — Earnings Intelligence is a single-period-over-period comparison (latest FY vs. the FY before it) focused on "what just happened"; Business Analysis is a 5-year CAGR/trend-consistency view focused on "what's the sustained trajectory." A one-off bad year and a genuine multi-year decline look identical in a two-period comparison but very different in a 5-year trend — the two tools are complementary, not redundant.
7. **"Why does Athena's revenue-vs-profit-growth observation say 'ahead of' or 'behind' rather than 'good' or 'bad'?"** — Because profit growth outpacing revenue growth (margin expansion) or lagging it (margin contraction) is a fact about the relationship between two numbers, not automatically a positive or negative judgment — a company deliberately reinvesting for growth might show margin contraction that's a strategic choice, not a weakness.
8. **"How would you explain a negative FCF Conversion ratio to a non-technical investor?"** — It means free cash flow and net income had opposite signs this period (e.g. positive cash generation despite a reported net loss, often from a large non-cash write-down) — not that anything improper happened, just that the accounting profit figure and the cash reality diverged this period, which is common and usually explainable by looking at what drove the net loss.
9. **"Why does Athena's percentChange function return null instead of a number when the prior period's value is zero or negative?"** — Because the result would be a technically-computable but practically misleading number (division by zero, or a sign-flipped percentage from a negative base that doesn't describe the actual business change) — Athena returns `null` and renders "Not available" rather than a number that looks precise but isn't meaningful.
10. **"A user asks why EPS doesn't show a signal (Improving/Stable/Deteriorating) like every other row. Why not?"** — EPS is only marked `available` when both periods report a value — Yahoo's per-share fields are among the sparsest in Athena's dataset, and a lone latest-period EPS with no verified prior-period comparison isn't reliable enough to attach a judgment to, unlike revenue/income/cash metrics which are still meaningful even as a single reported figure.

## Product Management interview questions

1. **"What's the core product question Sprint 12 answers, and how is it different from Sprints 10 and 11?"** — Sprint 10 answered "what's happening around the company" (News), Sprint 11 answered "what changed that deserves my attention" (Alerts), Sprint 12 answers "how did the company perform in its latest reported period, specifically" — a focused, comparison-first read of the most recent results, distinct from both a news feed and a threshold-triggered notification.
2. **"Why did you avoid charts entirely on the Earnings page?"** — The brief is explicit: avoid information overload, and only add a visualization if it clearly improves comprehension. A grouped comparison table already communicates every required metric precisely (exact values, exact deltas) — a chart would add visual complexity without adding information a careful reader couldn't already get from the table.
3. **"How did you decide the AI narration endpoint could be cut from this sprint without the feature feeling incomplete?"** — The brief itself frames AI narration as optional ("AI may optionally explain the scorecard"), and the deterministic API and page are fully functional and independently valuable without it — a user gets the complete comparison, signals, quality observations, and market reaction with or without a narrative summary layered on top. I confirmed this explicitly with a stakeholder decision point mid-sprint rather than assuming it.
4. **"Why does the Market Reaction section sometimes just say 'not available' instead of showing something?"** — Because Athena genuinely doesn't always have the data to answer honestly — if no Earnings-category news has been retrieved for a ticker yet, there's no reliable anchor date to measure a reaction from. Showing a fabricated or approximated number instead would trade honesty for the appearance of completeness, which the brief explicitly warns against across every section (missing data should say "Not available," never be estimated).
5. **"How would you pitch the AI narration feature as the next increment, now that it's designed but not built?"** — The design work (schema shape, evidence allow-list, grounding pattern) is already done and directly reuses Sprint 8's proven approach — implementing it is now a scoped, well-understood task rather than an open question, which makes it a low-risk next increment once there's a product reason to prioritize it over other work.
6. **"Why does Earnings Intelligence sit between Valuation and News in the navigation, not somewhere else?"** — It follows the brief's own suggested ordering and reflects the natural reading flow: a user has already seen the company's story (Overview/Financials/Analysis), its intrinsic value estimate (Valuation), and now wants the latest concrete results (Earnings) before checking what's currently being said about the company (News).
7. **"What would you cut first if this sprint had half the time?"** — Market Reaction — it's the one section built on a genuinely underspecified data source (a news-article-date proxy rather than a real event date), and the feature is still coherent and useful without it; the comparison table, signals, and quality observations are the load-bearing core.
8. **"How do you know the percentage-point vs. relative-percent distinction is actually being respected everywhere, not just where you remembered to apply it?"** — Structurally, not by memory — `marginPointChange` and `percentChange` are separate functions with separate call sites (`buildMarginMetric` vs. `buildVarianceMetric`), so a margin metric can never accidentally be routed through the relative-percent path; the test suite asserts the specific expected number (e.g. -1.7, not -5.6) for a known margin move, which would fail immediately if the wrong function were ever wired to the wrong metric.
9. **"What user research would most change this feature's design?"** — Whether the deliberately neutral tone of the quality observations ("moved in different directions") is read as helpfully calm or as unhelpfully vague by real users — that's a genuine open question this sprint made a judgment call on but didn't validate with actual users.
10. **"If a stakeholder asked why quarterly earnings weren't shipped despite being requested in the brief, what would you say?"** — Athena's financial data provider genuinely doesn't supply quarterly statements today — verified by inspecting the actual Yahoo/Twelve Data provider code, not assumed. Building the feature to only support annual data, but architecting the period-resolution layer so quarterly is a clean addition later (not a rewrite), is the honest trade-off versus either fabricating quarterly numbers or blocking the entire sprint on a data-source project.

## Engineering / AI interview questions

1. **"Walk through the one design decision in this sprint you couldn't just reuse from an earlier sprint."** — Market reaction's anchor date. Every other part of Sprint 12 reused an existing pattern (ratio formulas, alert thresholds, the News/Market service layers) — but nothing in Athena had ever needed an "earnings event date" before, because Alerts and Business Analysis both operate on fiscal years directly, never on a dated market event. I chose to anchor on the most recent Earnings-category news article and disclose that choice explicitly in the API response, rather than either fabricating a date or silently picking one without explanation.
2. **"How is `earnings.calculator.js` kept deterministic, and how do you know it actually is?"** — No `Date.now()`/`Math.random()`/network/database calls anywhere inside it — every function is a pure transformation of the two statement documents it's given. The test suite (`earnings.calculator.test.js`, 42 tests) asserts exact output values for fixed inputs; a non-deterministic function would make those assertions flaky, and they aren't.
3. **"Why does `earnings.signals.js` import thresholds from `alert.rules.js` instead of defining its own?"** — So a Deteriorating earnings signal and an Alert Engine trigger for the same underlying metric change always agree about what "significant" means — two independently-maintained threshold sets for the same concept is exactly the kind of quiet-drift risk that reuse eliminates. The one threshold with no existing analog (raw growth-rate magnitude) is a new, documented number specifically because nothing else in the codebase measured that particular thing.
4. **"You designed the `marketReaction` response shape in Phase 4, before the module that computes it existed in Phase 7. What made that possible, and what would have happened if you'd guessed the shape wrong?"** — `earnings.formatter.js` and the frontend's `MarketReactionCard` were both written against a documented `{available, reason, basis, anchorDate, oneDayReturnPercent, fiveDayReturnPercent}` contract before the real computation existed, using an explicit "unavailable" stub in the meantime. Because the module I built in Phase 7 was designed to conform to that same contract, wiring it in required zero changes to the formatter or frontend. Had the shape been wrong, the fix would have been localized to the formatter's mapping, not a frontend rewrite — the cost of a wrong guess was bounded by the layering itself.
5. **"Why was the AI narration endpoint fully designed in a document but not implemented, rather than just skipped entirely?"** — Because the design decisions (schema shape, evidence allow-list, grounding pattern) are the genuinely hard part of adding AI to a deterministic feature, and they're best made deliberately against a proven precedent (Sprint 8) rather than either rushed alongside a large sprint or left as an unexamined "maybe someday." Writing it down turns a vague future feature into a scoped, immediately-buildable task.
