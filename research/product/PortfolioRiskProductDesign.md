# Portfolio Risk Product Design

## 1. Why is portfolio tracking alone insufficient?

Sprint 9 answers "what do I own, and how has each position done." That's necessary but incomplete: two portfolios can have identical total value and identical total return while carrying completely different risk profiles - one spread across ten uncorrelated positions in five sectors, the other concentrated in three highly-correlated technology stocks. Cost basis, current value, and return - Sprint 9's whole vocabulary - simply cannot express that difference, because none of those numbers describe how the portfolio behaves as a *system*, only how each position behaves in isolation. A user who only ever sees per-holding numbers has no way to notice that their portfolio, as a whole, moves far more violently than any individual holding suggests - Sprint 14 exists to close exactly that gap.

## 2. Why do users need risk context, not just performance context?

Performance answers "how did I do"; risk context answers "how much could this vary, and why." Both matter, but they answer different questions a user actually has: someone deciding whether to add a fourth technology stock needs to know their portfolio is already 70% technology-exposed far more than they need another return percentage - the return number won't tell them that, but Exposure will. Risk metrics exist to make visible what performance alone hides: the shape and behavior of the portfolio, not just its current scoreboard.

## 3. Why does exposure matter specifically?

Exposure answers a question concentration alone can't: not just "how much is in my biggest position" but "what *kind* of business is my money actually in." A portfolio can pass a low-concentration test (ten different tickers, none dominant) while still being an effective single-sector bet if all ten are semiconductor companies - see PortfolioDiversification.md for the full "five holdings, one sector" example. Without exposure, a user has no way to notice that failure mode; concentration alone would tell them everything looks fine.

## 4. Why should concentration be displayed neutrally?

Because concentration is a fact about portfolio structure, not a verdict about whether that structure is wise - a concentrated, high-conviction bet in a company a user has researched deeply is a legitimate, deliberate strategy, not a mistake to be corrected. The moment Athena renders "your top holding is 40% of your portfolio" with warning-red color or the word "risky," it stops being an analytics tool and starts making an implicit recommendation ("this is too much") that it has no basis to make - Athena doesn't know the user's risk tolerance, time horizon, or conviction level, only the number. Neutral language and neutral color are the mechanism that keeps the product in its lane: "Your largest holding represents 34% of portfolio value" states a fact a user can act on or ignore as they see fit; "your portfolio is dangerously concentrated" makes a judgment Athena isn't positioned to make.

## 5. Why must assumptions be visible?

Because almost every historical number in this sprint is an estimate built on an assumption Athena had to choose (current holdings applied backward over historical prices - see PortfolioCalculationAssumptions.md), and a user interpreting a Sharpe Ratio or a volatility figure without knowing that changes what the number means to them. A volatility figure presented as "this portfolio's actual historical behavior" is a different, stronger claim than the same number presented as "an estimate of how today's holdings would have behaved" - and only the second claim is true. The Assumptions panel isn't a legal disclaimer bolted on for cover; it's the information a user needs to correctly calibrate how much weight to put on every other number on the page. Burying it in a tooltip would technically satisfy "disclosed somewhere" while failing the actual goal, which is that a user reads it.

## 6. Why must portfolio analytics never become investment advice?

Because Athena's credibility as an *intelligence* platform depends on a clean line between "here is what your portfolio's data shows" and "here is what you should do about it" - the moment Athena crosses into recommending BUY/SELL/HOLD/REDUCE/INCREASE, it takes on the responsibility (and liability, and expertise bar) of a financial advisor, which is a fundamentally different product with fundamentally different obligations. It also, subtly, would make every other number in this sprint less trustworthy: a user who sees Athena editorialize on concentration ("consider diversifying") will reasonably start wondering whether the *volatility* number or the *beta* number are similarly massaged toward some house opinion, rather than being cold, reproducible calculations. Strict neutrality on every surface is what lets a user trust the math is just math.

## 7. Why are we deferring portfolio optimization?

Because optimization (suggesting *how* to change a portfolio to hit a target risk/return profile) requires knowing things Athena doesn't have and shouldn't assume: a user's risk tolerance, time horizon, tax situation, liquidity needs, and goals outside the app entirely. Describing a portfolio's current characteristics is a well-scoped, honest problem Athena can solve correctly with the data it has; prescribing changes to it is a different, much harder problem that would require either asking users for information Athena doesn't currently collect, or making assumptions on their behalf that could be wrong in ways that matter far more than a mislabeled chart. Sprint 14 explicitly stops at description - see the sprint brief's REBALANCING section for the same boundary applied to historical weight assumptions.

## 8. How does this improve Athena's value proposition?

It moves Athena from "a place that tells you what you own" to "a place that helps you understand how what you own behaves as a system" - a materially deeper form of value that's much harder for a simple brokerage summary page to replicate. Sprint 9's numbers (cost basis, gain/loss) are things most brokerage apps already show; volatility, beta, Sharpe, drawdown, sector exposure, and correlation are the kind of analysis that used to require either a finance background or a paid tool, now available for free on holdings a user already tracks in Athena. It's also a natural retention driver in the same spirit as Sprint 9's watchlist (see WatchlistAndPortfolioProductDesign.md Q2): risk metrics change as markets move and as a user's holdings change, giving them a reason to check back beyond just "did my return go up."

## 9. What user behavior do we expect?

That most users check Performance and Risk more often than Correlation - the first two answer questions users already have in mind ("how am I doing," "how risky is this"), while Correlation answers a question that requires already understanding *why* it matters, which is a smaller, more sophisticated audience. That the Assumptions panel gets read disproportionately by users who've noticed a number that surprised them (a Sharpe Ratio that seems off, a volatility that seems high) rather than being read top-to-bottom on every visit - which is exactly why it needs to be one click away from every section that depends on it, not a single page users have to remember to seek out. And that Exposure and Concentration together are what most effectively prompt a user to click through to a specific holding's Company Dashboard (Sprint 5) or Industry Intelligence page (Sprint 13) - "I didn't realize I was this concentrated in one sector" is a much stronger prompt to go investigate a specific company than a raw return number is.

## 10. What user research should validate these assumptions?

Whether users can correctly explain, in their own words, what "current-weight approximation" means for the numbers they're looking at after reading the Assumptions panel - the direct test of whether Q5's visibility goal is actually met, or whether the panel is present but not actually understood. Whether users who see a high concentration or high single-sector exposure figure describe it neutrally when asked about it ("a lot of my money is in tech") versus with unprompted alarm ("is this bad?") - testing whether the neutral-language design (Q4/Q6) is actually landing as intended, or whether any large number reads as implicitly bad regardless of framing. Whether Sharpe Ratio and Beta are understood or mostly ignored/misread by users without a finance background - these are the two most jargon-dense metrics in the sprint, and it's worth knowing whether they need companion explanatory text, a glossary link, or are simply not for every user. And whether the separate Correlation/Exposure/Concentration presentation (rather than one composite "diversification score," per PortfolioDiversification.md Q10) is actually preferred by users, or whether most people want the single number Athena deliberately chose not to build.

## Potential product metrics

Portfolio Analytics page views (and repeat views, distinct from one-time curiosity), Risk section interaction (expanding drawdown detail, hovering a stat), Exposure section interaction (clicking through to a sector-heavy holding), Correlation matrix usage (a strong signal for the "sophisticated subset of users" hypothesis in Q9), Assumptions panel expansion/read rate, and Analytics-to-Company-Dashboard or Analytics-to-Industry-Intelligence click-through rate (the cross-feature funnel referenced in Q9). None of these should be chased for their own sake - they're diagnostic instruments for the hypotheses above, not targets the feature was built to hit.
