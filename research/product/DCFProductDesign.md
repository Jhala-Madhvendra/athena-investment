# DCF Product Design

## 1. Why does DCF belong in Athena?

Every prior sprint answered "how is this company doing" — financial statements (Sprint 1), ratios (Sprint 2), business health and trends (Sprint 3), how the market is currently pricing the stock (Sprint 4), and a unified summary of all of it (Sprint 5). None of them answered the question every one of those sprints was implicitly building toward: *is the current price reasonable, given what the business can actually generate in cash?* DCF is the first feature that connects Athena's fundamental analysis (Sprints 1–3) to its market data (Sprint 4) into an actual valuation judgment. Without it, Athena is a very good research terminal that stops exactly at the question an investor cares about most.

## 2. Why must assumptions be visible?

Because a DCF's output is entirely a function of its inputs, and inputs that are hidden are inputs a user can't evaluate, challenge, or trust. A tool that shows "$96/share" with no visibility into whether that came from a 2% or a 12% assumed growth rate is asking for blind trust in a model the user can't inspect — the opposite of what a serious analytical tool should ask for. Athena's answer is structural, not just a UI nicety: every single assumption in the DCF form carries a labeled source (`historical`, `derived`, `market`, `illustrative_default`, `required_user_input`) rendered as a visually distinct badge, so a user glancing at the form immediately sees which numbers are the company's own reported history, which are computed from that history, which are live market data, which are common textbook conventions Athena is explicitly not claiming as fact, and which Athena refuses to guess at all.

## 3. Why is a black-box valuation bad product design?

Because it optimizes for a false sense of confidence over genuine understanding. A black box that outputs "$96, undervalued" is easier to build and easier to glance at than a transparent model — but it fails the exact moment a user asks "why," which for a financial decision is almost immediately. Worse, a black box invites a specific kind of misuse: users start trusting the number *because* it's presented cleanly, not because they understand what's driving it — the presentation quality substitutes for actual comprehension. Athena's entire Sprint 6 design — the labeled assumption sources, the full per-year calculation waterfall (revenue → EBIT → NOPAT → FCFF → discounted → summed), the WACC breakdown, the scenario comparison, the sensitivity table — exists specifically to make every step of "how did we get to this number" inspectable, because a valuation tool's job is to inform judgment, not replace it.

## 4. Why is scenario analysis useful?

Because a single DCF number invites the question "but what if the business doesn't perform exactly as forecast" — and rather than making the user manually re-run the calculation three times to answer it, Athena answers it automatically alongside the base case. Bear/Base/Bull, framed as named business-performance narratives rather than abstract parameter changes, gives a user an intuitive sense of the *range* a reasonable disagreement about the company's execution implies — which is a more honest and more useful artifact than a single confident-looking number, and closer to how an experienced analyst actually thinks about a valuation.

## 5. Why is sensitivity analysis necessary?

Because scenario analysis bounds *business-performance* uncertainty, but a DCF is also — separately, and often more dramatically — sensitive to its *valuation-mechanics* assumptions (WACC, terminal growth), which most users have far less intuition for than "will this company grow faster or slower than expected." The sensitivity table exists to make that second, less-intuitive kind of uncertainty just as visible as the first: a user can see, in one glance, how much the output moves across a plausible WACC and terminal-growth range, without needing to understand *why* the Gordon Growth formula is so sensitive near its boundary (TerminalValue.md) — the table shows the consequence directly, even if the underlying math stays implicit.

## 6. How should a product communicate uncertainty?

Not with a single disclaimer buried in fine print, and not by refusing to give a number at all — both are failure modes. Athena's approach layers multiple, complementary signals: an explicit, always-visible disclaimer text on every result ("DCF valuation is highly sensitive to assumptions and should not be interpreted as a guaranteed future price"); visually distinct labeling of which inputs are facts versus assumptions versus pure defaults; a scenario range instead of one number; and a sensitivity table that makes the model's own volatility to its two most consequential inputs directly visible. The goal is a user who finishes looking at the page with an accurate sense of *how much to trust this number and why*, not a user who either blindly trusts a clean-looking figure or dismisses the whole feature because "it's just a guess."

## 7. Why does Athena never give Buy/Sell recommendations?

Because a recommendation collapses a nuanced, assumption-dependent analytical output into a binary that implies a certainty the underlying model doesn't have — and because Athena is explicitly positioned as an *analytical tool*, not a financial adviser, a distinction with real regulatory and trust implications, not just a stylistic one. A DCF's "valuation gap" is a prompt for further investigation (which specific assumption is driving the gap? does the sensitivity table's whole range still favor one conclusion?), not a verdict — collapsing it into "Buy" throws away exactly the nuance the rest of the feature works hard to preserve. This isn't just a copywriting choice: it's enforced structurally (no code path in the DCF pipeline maps a valuation gap to a recommendation string) and verified by tests at both the API and rendered-page level specifically so it can never silently regress.

## 8. What user research would validate the DCF experience?

Whether users actually read and understand the source-labeling badges, or scroll past them the same way people scroll past terms-and-conditions text — if the latter, the visible-assumptions design isn't achieving its actual goal even though it's technically present. Whether users adjust the pre-filled assumptions at all before calculating, or always just click through with the defaults — if it's always the latter, the "illustrative, not authoritative" framing may not be landing, and Athena may be functionally producing one-size-fits-all numbers despite the UI implying otherwise. Whether the sensitivity table gets used to genuinely stress-test a conclusion, or gets ignored in favor of the single headline intrinsic-value figure. And directly: whether users come away from a DCF result correctly describing it as "one model's estimate under stated assumptions" rather than "what the stock is really worth" — the single most important comprehension check for this feature.

## 9. What metrics would measure whether users find DCF useful?

- **Assumption-edit rate**: what fraction of DCF calculations use at least one user-modified value versus 100% defaults — a proxy for whether users are engaging with the model or just clicking through it.
- **Scenario/sensitivity engagement**: whether users who view a base DCF result also scroll to and interact with the Bull/Bear cards or the sensitivity table, versus stopping at the headline number — low engagement here would suggest the uncertainty-communication design isn't reaching users who most need it.
- **Return usage on the same ticker**: whether users come back and re-run a DCF for a company they've already valued (with different assumptions) — a signal the tool is being used as a genuine exploratory instrument rather than a one-shot lookup.
- **Correlation between valuation gap and next action** (if instrumented downstream): whether a large gap correlates with any observable follow-up behavior (reading more tabs, exporting/sharing) — evidence the number is prompting investigation rather than being taken as a final answer.

## Product Management interview questions

**Q1: A stakeholder asks why Athena doesn't just show a "Buy/Sell" badge like several competitor tools do — wouldn't that be more useful to users?**
A: More useful in the narrow sense of being easier to glance at, but it would misrepresent what a DCF actually produces — an estimate under stated, editable assumptions, not a settled verdict. A Buy/Sell badge implies a certainty the model doesn't have, and creates real risk: users acting on a recommendation Athena's own data doesn't actually support with that level of confidence. The valuation-gap framing preserves the nuance (how big is the gap, under which assumptions, how does it hold up across the sensitivity range) that a binary badge would necessarily discard — and Athena is positioned as an analytical tool, not an adviser, which is a distinction worth protecting deliberately, not eroding for a superficial UX win.

**Q2: How would you prioritize what to build next for the DCF feature given limited engineering time?**
A: I'd want the assumption-edit-rate and scenario/sensitivity-engagement metrics first (Section 9) before committing to anything — but my working hypothesis is per-year assumption overrides (the engine and API already support per-year arrays; only the UI currently applies one flat rate across the whole forecast). If users are engaging deeply with the base assumptions, that's the most direct way to let them express a more nuanced view (e.g., "growth is strong for two years, then decelerates") without leaving the tool.

**Q3: The sprint explicitly avoids fabricating certain assumptions (Pre-Tax Cost of Debt has no default at all) while pre-filling others with "illustrative" values (Equity Risk Premium, Terminal Growth). Isn't that inconsistent?**
A: It's a deliberate, principled line, not an inconsistency: Equity Risk Premium and Terminal Growth are broad, commonly-cited market-level conventions with genuine reference points in the finance literature — pre-filling them with a clearly-labeled illustrative figure lowers friction without fabricating anything company-specific. Pre-Tax Cost of Debt has no equivalent market-level convention — it's inherently company-specific (this company's actual borrowing cost), and Athena has no data source that's even a reasonable proxy for it. Guessing there wouldn't be a helpful default; it would be presenting a fabricated number as if it meant something about this specific company.

**Q4: How would you know if the labeled-assumption-sources design is actually working, versus just adding visual clutter?**
A: Primarily via the user research in Section 8 — specifically, whether users can correctly describe, after using the feature, which numbers in their DCF were company facts versus their own judgment calls. If they can't, the badges are decoration, not communication, regardless of how principled the underlying data-sourcing distinction is. I'd pair that qualitative check with the assumption-edit-rate metric: if edit rates are near zero even after confirming users *can* read the labels, that's evidence the defaults are landing as "good enough to trust," which is a different (and also useful) signal, not necessarily a failure.

**Q5: What's the biggest product risk in this feature as shipped, and how would you mitigate it?**
A: That a user treats the headline Intrinsic Value / Share number as authoritative despite every design choice working against that outcome — disclaimers and labels can be skimmed past, and a clean-looking dollar figure is inherently persuasive regardless of the caveats around it. The mitigation isn't more or louder disclaimer text (diminishing returns, risk of "warning fatigue"); it's making the *engagement path* itself require confronting the uncertainty — for instance, defaulting the page to show the sensitivity table and scenario range prominently alongside the headline number (already done) rather than behind an extra scroll or click, so the range is unavoidable context, not an optional deep-dive very few users reach.
