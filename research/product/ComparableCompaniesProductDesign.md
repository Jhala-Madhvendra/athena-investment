# Comparable Companies Product Design

## 1. Why do users need relative valuation, given Athena already has DCF?

Because DCF and Comps answer genuinely different investor questions, and an investor who only ever gets an intrinsic-value answer is missing half the picture. DCF answers "what should this business be worth, based on its own cash flows" — a question that's entirely blind to what the market is actually doing right now. Comps answers "what is the market currently paying for businesses like this one" — a question DCF structurally cannot answer, because it never looks at another company. A real investor asks both: is this cheap relative to its own fundamentals (DCF), and is it cheap relative to how its peers are priced today (Comps)? Shipping only one leaves a genuine analytical gap Athena's own Sprint 6 DCF work implicitly promised to eventually fill.

## 2. Why should peer selection initially remain user-controlled?

Because the alternative — Athena silently choosing peers on the user's behalf — fails in the worst possible way: confidently. An automatic "these are the comparable companies" list, even if wrong, carries the implicit authority of "the system decided this," and a user is far less likely to scrutinize a peer group the tool picked for them than one they picked themselves. Athena's current data (sector/industry tags, market cap, revenue) is nowhere near rich enough to make that judgment reliably — it can't distinguish two companies with the same sector label but entirely different business models, growth rates, or capital intensity (`research/finance/PeerSelection.md`). An empty peer list a human has to fill in is a worse *first impression* than a ranked suggestion list, but it is a much safer one — it forces exactly the scrutiny a comparability judgment actually requires.

## 3. Why should the application show peer data before the implied valuation?

Because an implied value per share is only trustworthy to the extent the peer data behind it is trustworthy, and a user who never sees that underlying data has no way to catch a bad peer, a stale multiple, or a peer with missing financials before it silently shapes their conclusion. Athena's page order is deliberate: Target Company → Peer Selection → Peer Comparison Table → Trading Multiples → Peer Statistics → *then* Implied Valuation → Valuation Range → DCF Comparison. By the time a user reaches the headline range, they've already seen exactly which peers went in, which of their multiples were valid versus excluded and why, and what the peer group's actual spread looks like — the same "show your work before the answer" discipline Sprint 6 established for DCF's calculation waterfall, applied here to peer selection instead of a cash-flow forecast.

## 4. Why should the calculation never be hidden?

Because a hidden calculation is unfalsifiable — a user can't ask "does this make sense" of a number they can't see the inputs to. Every implied value Athena shows traces back, visibly, to a specific peer statistic and a specific target metric (the Implied Valuation table shows both, per multiple, not just the final per-share figure), exactly mirroring why Sprint 6 never presented DCF's Intrinsic Value Per Share without also showing the WACC breakdown and the FCFF waterfall beneath it. A valuation tool that asks for trust without showing its work is asking for the wrong kind of trust.

## 5. Why is a valuation range better than a single number?

Because a single number implies a precision that five different multiples, applied to five different peer statistics, simply don't actually agree on — and averaging them into one figure would manufacture false consensus out of genuine disagreement between methodologies. Athena's Valuation Range (Low/Median/High across every applicable methodology) preserves that disagreement as information: a narrow range says the methodologies broadly agree; a wide one says they don't, and that gap itself is worth investigating (which multiple is the outlier, and why) rather than being averaged away before the user ever sees it. The sprint brief is explicit that Athena must not auto-select a "best" number or default to a simple average — the range *is* the honest answer.

## 6. How should uncertainty be communicated here?

Layered, the same way Sprint 6 did it for DCF, not with one disclaimer buried at the bottom: the Valuation Range makes cross-methodology disagreement visible up front; the Peer Statistics table shows each multiple's actual spread (Min/Max/Mean/Median/P25/P75) rather than collapsing it before the user sees it; excluded observations are shown with their specific reason rather than silently dropped; and the DCF-vs-Comps comparison section explicitly states that a gap between the two is *expected*, not a sign either one is broken. The goal, as with DCF, is a user who finishes the page with an accurate sense of how much to trust the number and why — not one who either blindly trusts a clean-looking figure or dismisses the whole analysis as unreliable.

## 7. What user research would validate this feature?

Whether users actually evaluate the candidate peers Athena surfaces (checking sector, industry, market cap, revenue before adding one) or just add the first few results without scrutiny — if it's the latter, the "candidates only, not recommendations" framing (`PeerSelection.md`) isn't landing, and Athena is functionally producing auto-selected peer groups despite the UI implying otherwise. Whether users notice and act on the `limitation` messaging on the peer-candidate list, or scroll past it the same way DCF's source badges risk being scrolled past (a known open question from `DCFProductDesign.md`'s own Section 8). Whether users who see a wide DCF-vs-Comps gap investigate *why*, or just anchor on whichever number is more favorable to a view they already held. And directly: whether users can correctly describe a Comps result afterward as "what the market is paying for similar companies" rather than "what this stock is worth" — the same category of comprehension check DCF's product design already prioritizes, applied to the opposite kind of valuation.

## 8. What future automation could improve peer selection?

A genuine similarity-scoring model — one that goes beyond sector/industry strings to weigh actual business-model taxonomy, revenue-scale proximity, margin-structure similarity, and growth-trajectory alignment — could reasonably rank candidates rather than just list them, *once* Athena has the underlying data to make that ranking trustworthy (see `PeerSelectionEngine.md`'s interview answer on exactly this). Even short of full automation, smaller improvements are available without over-claiming confidence: flagging when a candidate's revenue scale is an order of magnitude off from the target's, or surfacing analyst-defined "peer group" data from a market-data provider (if Athena ever integrates one) as an additional *candidate source*, not an automatic decision. The through-line for all of these: surface more information to help the human judge, don't replace the human judgment.

## 9. How does this feature complement DCF?

They're deliberately kept visually and structurally adjacent — same Valuation section, one tab switch apart, with the DCF result available to the Comps tab for a direct side-by-side comparison — specifically so a user treats them as two lenses on the same question, not two disconnected features. Where DCF forces explicit, editable assumptions about a company's own future, Comps forces explicit, editable assumptions about which companies are truly comparable to it. A user who runs both and finds them roughly aligned has real corroborating evidence; a user who finds them far apart has a genuinely interesting question to dig into — and Athena's job, in both directions, is to make that comparison possible and legible, never to resolve it on the user's behalf.

## Product Management interview questions

**Q1: A stakeholder asks why Athena doesn't just average the five implied values into one "the" valuation number — wouldn't that be simpler for users?**
A: Simpler to glance at, but it would manufacture false precision out of genuine methodological disagreement. The five multiples aren't five noisy measurements of the same underlying truth — they're five different lenses (equity vs. enterprise basis, earnings vs. revenue vs. book value) that can legitimately diverge for real economic reasons (see `research/finance/ValuationMultiples.md`). Averaging them treats that divergence as noise to be smoothed over instead of information to be shown, which is exactly backwards for an analytical tool. The sprint brief is explicit on this point for a reason: showing the range, and letting a user see *which* methodology is driving a high or low estimate, is more useful than a single blended number that hides that signal.

**Q2: Why not just auto-suggest peers based on sector, the way several competitor tools do — wouldn't that reduce friction for new users?**
A: It would reduce friction while adding a much more dangerous failure mode: a confidently-wrong peer group presented as if the system had done the comparability judgment for the user. Athena's current data (sector tags, market cap, revenue) genuinely isn't rich enough to make that judgment reliably — two companies in the same sector can have entirely different business models and margin structures. An empty list a user has to fill in themselves is worse onboarding, but it's honest onboarding: it doesn't let a user skip the one judgment call (is this peer actually comparable?) that determines whether the entire downstream analysis means anything.

**Q3: How would you prioritize what to build next for this feature given limited engineering time?**
A: I'd want the user-research signals from Section 7 first — specifically, whether users are genuinely evaluating candidates before adding them, and whether they engage with the DCF-vs-Comps comparison at all. My working hypothesis, though, is that peer-list persistence (so a user doesn't have to reselect the same peer group every session) is the highest-value near-term addition — it's explicitly out of scope for this sprint (see `research/engineering/DynamicValuationCalculation.md`'s "Data Storage" reasoning) precisely because it raises real staleness questions that deserve their own design pass, not because it isn't valuable.

**Q4: The sprint explicitly forbids showing Buy/Sell language, even labeled as "analytical, not a recommendation" — isn't that overly cautious given Athena already has that same rule for DCF?**
A: It's consistency, not extra caution — Athena's entire premise as an *analytical tool, not a financial adviser* would be undermined by holding DCF to one standard and Comps to a looser one. A Comps "implied value" carries exactly the same risk of being misread as a price target that a DCF "intrinsic value" does, and arguably a bit more, since Comps' inputs (a hand-picked peer group) are more visibly under the user's own control — which could tempt a "well, I chose the peers, so this number is more 'mine' and more trustworthy" framing that's just as misleading as trusting a DCF number blindly.

**Q5: What's the biggest product risk in this feature as shipped, and how would you mitigate it?**
A: That a user selects a peer group carelessly (loosely related companies added quickly to get past the two-peer minimum) and then reads the resulting Valuation Range with the same confidence they'd give a carefully-constructed one — the range display communicates *disagreement between methodologies* well, but it doesn't and can't communicate *whether the peer group itself was well-chosen*. The mitigation isn't a stronger warning (diminishing returns, same warning-fatigue risk `DCFProductDesign.md` already flags); it's surfacing more peer-quality signal directly in the peer selection UI itself — e.g., visibly flagging a wide revenue-scale mismatch between a candidate and the target at the moment of adding it, so the friction happens at the decision point, not after the fact in a results page the user may not re-examine critically.
