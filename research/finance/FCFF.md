# Free Cash Flow to the Firm (FCFF)

## 1. Definition

FCFF is the cash a company generates from its core operations that is available to **everyone who has a claim on the business** — both debt holders and equity holders — before any financing decisions (how much debt to carry, how much to pay in interest, whether to buy back stock) are made. It is the cash flow Athena's DCF engine discounts to arrive at Enterprise Value.

## 2. The formula

Athena uses the top-down-from-EBIT formulation:

```
FCFF = EBIT × (1 − Tax Rate) + D&A − CapEx − Change in NWC
```

Where NOPAT (Net Operating Profit After Tax) = `EBIT × (1 − Tax Rate)`.

## 3. Intuition

Start from EBIT — operating profit before interest — because interest is a *financing* cost, not an *operating* one, and FCFF needs to be capital-structure-neutral. Tax the operating profit as if there were no debt (NOPAT), because the actual tax bill already reflects the interest tax shield, which is a financing side-effect Athena doesn't want mixed into an unlevered number. Then add back D&A (a non-cash accounting charge that reduced EBIT but never left the bank), subtract CapEx (real cash that left the bank to buy/maintain assets, but never touched EBIT), and subtract the increase in net working capital (cash tied up funding growth — more receivables and inventory than payables — that also never touched EBIT). What's left is cash generated by the business itself, untouched by how it's financed.

## 4. Why investors use it

FCFF is what you discount at WACC to get Enterprise Value — the value of the whole operating business, independent of its current debt load. That independence is the point: it lets you value a company the same way whether it's financed with 80% debt or 0% debt, then separately account for the actual capital structure by subtracting net debt. Comparing two companies' FCFE or net income directly is comparing apples financed with debt to oranges financed with equity; comparing FCFF is not.

## 5. Assumptions

- EBIT is a reasonable proxy for pure operating income (Athena uses `incomeStatement.operatingIncome` directly — see Common Mistakes below for what this glosses over).
- The tax rate applied is the rate that would apply to operating profit in isolation, not the company's actual (post-interest-shield) effective rate — Athena uses effective historical rate as a *default* for forecasting, but treats it as an assumption the user can override, not a computed fact.
- CapEx and D&A as reported are representative of an ongoing, steady-state level of reinvestment (a one-off asset sale or acquisition-heavy year distorts this).

## 6. Limitations

FCFF can legitimately be negative for a fast-growing or heavily-investing company in a given year (see Athena's own regression test: `capexPercentRevenue` high enough that `forecastFCFF` produces a negative first-year FCFF is a *valid*, not broken, result). A single year of FCFF says little; the DCF's real signal is the multi-year trajectory plus the terminal value, which is why Athena always shows the full per-year waterfall (`FCFFForecastTable.jsx`), never a single number.

## 7. How Athena implements it

`backend/valuation/dcf/dcf.formulas.js`'s `fcff()`:

```js
const fcff = ({ ebit, taxRate, depreciationAndAmortization, capitalExpenditure, changeInNWC }) => {
    const nopatValue = nopat(ebit, taxRate);
    // ...
    return nopatValue + depreciationAndAmortization - capitalExpenditure - changeInNWC;
};
```

Historically, `dcf.engine.js`'s `calculateHistoricalFCFF()` reads `EBIT` from `incomeStatement.operatingIncome`, tax rate from `effectiveTaxRate(pretaxIncome, taxProvision)`, D&A and CapEx from the cash flow statement, and Change in NWC from year-over-year `(currentAssets − currentLiabilities)`. For the forecast, every input is projected from revenue via user-set percent-of-revenue assumptions (`dcf.engine.js`'s `forecastFCFF()`).

**Free Cash Flow vs. FCFF vs. FCFE vs. Operating Cash Flow — the distinction Athena is careful about:**

| Metric | Formula | What it represents |
|---|---|---|
| Operating Cash Flow | From the cash flow statement directly | Cash from operations, *after* interest (since it starts from Net Income) |
| "Free Cash Flow" (Sprint 1 ratio card) | `OCF − CapEx` | A common but *levered* FCF proxy — reflects the actual capital structure |
| **FCFF** (this doc) | `EBIT(1−t) + D&A − CapEx − ΔNWC` | Unlevered — cash available to all capital providers, capital-structure-neutral |
| FCFE | `FCFF − Interest(1−t) + Net Borrowing` (see FCFE.md) | Cash available to equity holders only, *after* debt service |

The existing Sprint 1 "Free Cash Flow" ratio (`ratio.formulas.js`) is `OCF − CapEx` — a reasonable, widely-used quick metric, but it is **not** FCFF, and it is not what Athena's DCF discounts. Conflating the two would be a real error: discounting `OCF − CapEx` at WACC mixes a levered cash flow with an unlevered discount rate.

## 8. Common mistakes

- **Treating "Free Cash Flow" (OCF − CapEx) as interchangeable with FCFF.** They differ by the after-tax interest impact embedded in Net Income (which OCF starts from) — for a highly levered company, the gap can be large.
- **Getting the CapEx sign wrong.** Real-world data (and Yahoo specifically) reports CapEx as a *negative* outflow. Athena hit this directly: an early version of both `dcf.engine.js`'s historical FCFF calculation and the pre-existing Sprint 1 `ratio.formulas.js` FCF card subtracted the raw signed value, which — because subtracting a negative *adds* it — silently inflated the result. Both are now fixed with an explicit `Math.abs()` normalization, and it's covered by regression tests in both modules specifically because it's easy to get wrong silently.
- **Using EBIT and "operating income" interchangeably without checking for non-operating items.** Athena does this too (documented, not hidden) — for most companies the gap is small, but a company with large one-off items inside "Operating Income" will distort FCFF.

## 9. Interview questions

*"Walk me through why you'd discount FCFF at WACC but never at cost of equity."* — WACC blends the cost of capital across the company's actual financing mix (debt + equity); FCFF is deliberately unlevered (calculated before any financing effects). Discounting an unlevered cash flow at a blended-but-still-capital-structure-aware rate is the correct pairing that produces Enterprise Value. Cost of equity is the return required by equity holders specifically — pairing it with FCFF would double-count leverage in one direction (FCFF ignores it) and not at all in the discount rate's applicable scope (Ke only prices equity risk), producing a value that's neither EV nor equity value.

*"A company's CapEx line in your data source is reported as -12,715,000,000. What do you do with that?"* — Take the absolute value before subtracting it in the FCFF formula; the negative sign is the source's outflow convention, not a signal to add it back. (This is a real bug Athena shipped and fixed during Sprint 6 — see the Common Mistakes section above.)
